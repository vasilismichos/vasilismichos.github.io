### Theme vowels

_Current focus._ Various positions have been taken in relation to theme vowels across languages, with the most prominent theoretical family arguing that they must be coming in at some post-syntactic level of representation. In examining the data of verbal morphology comparatively in Greko and Standard Modern Greek, I am arguing that theme vowels may well be the realization of (ordinary and binary) morphosyntactic features that occupy a syntactic position. This is inspired by recent work of my advisor, [Neil Myler](https://sites.google.com/site/neilmylerlinguist/), on Romance conjugation class features.

Poster at the Boston Morphology Workshop: [fragment](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1vjUFd5sFmmH5mCa9zHxSsoO-106vVzNY/view?usp=sharing), [poster](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1gNziPCK1hWw3qX00uSbkSfAPHLDBsq2Z/view?usp=sharing).

### Polydefiniteness

Relatively few (but unrelated) languages allow weirdly shaped constructions, like _the big the car the blue_. I examine the semantics and pragmatics of these with [Liz Coppock](https://ling.bu.edu/people/coppock/) for my second qualifying paper.

### Compounding

I've studied compounding in Greek and varieties thereof. I've argued that VO deverbal compounds (_kill-joy_) are perfectively marked to ensure categorial distinctness between the first compound member and the nominalizing suffix, preventing a possible FOFC violation. Here is the [proceedings paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391769055_A_Final-Over-Final_Constraint_effect_in_Modern_Greek_compounding), as presented at the 44th AMGL.

I've also worked towards creating a Northern Pomo 'compoundicon'. This work studies syntactic dependencies between sub-word constituents and their prosody in the dormant language of Northern Pomo. This year, I'm enhancing the documentation and revitalization tools available at [northernpomolanguagetools.com](https://northernpomolanguagetools.com/).

### Cliticization

I take interest in some under-studied cliticization patterns of (mostly Northern Greek) dialects. These exhibit a great deal of variation and differ significantly in their clausal spine from that of standard varieties. I've argued that mesoclisis (a phenomenon in which a weak object pronoun splits the verbal root from its inflectional suffix) can be understood as syntactic enclisis plus a post-syntactic rule known as Generalized Reduplication. Here is a [pre-print of the proceedings paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391902646_Mesoclisis_across_Modern_Greek_dialects_Proclisis_Enclisis_or_Neither), as presented at the 10th MGDLT.

### Implicit complements

My MA thesis focused on a typology of implicit complements in Modern Greek and its implications for the manner/result complementarity hypothesis. I've argued that the diagnostic of direct object omission which prior literature uses to distinguish between <span style="font-variant: small-caps">manner</span> and <span style="font-variant: small-caps">result</span> can only be applied correctly if a set of syntactic and pragmatic restrictions are met. Here is the [full thesis](https://ikee.lib.auth.gr/record/341289?ln=en), also available [as a pdf](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1ILJrbkjH8j6JnY-AKTrusV_RuKgFDyJh/view?usp=sharing).

### Whistled Greek

There are many (under-studied) whistled languages across the globe, and one of them is whistled in Southern Evoia, in a village of about 30 residents called Antias. Together with colleague [Andre Batchelder-Schwab](https://andreschwab.com/) and Professor [Jon Barnes](https://ling.bu.edu/people/barnes/), we constructed seven experiments that either tested previously made claims in the literature or explored new ways in which the whistled speech showed correlates of phonological processes (e.g. stress shift, palatalization). Part of this research has also benefited the local community: after three fieldwork trips we were able to help organize and run a revitalization school in the village. We found strong phonetic correlates for things that you can find out too, if you [email me](mailto:michos@bu.edu)! See our [Interspeech 2025 paper](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2025/batchelderschwab25_interspeech.html), and check out [this story from Scientific American](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PTHzOag8msM).

### Possession in Ende

Ende, a Pahoturi River language, displays some fancy alternations when marking possession. I've worked with Prof. [Kate Lindsey](https://katelynnlindsey.weebly.com/) for a Research Fellowship and disentangled some of them, by comparing and contrasting these intra- and cross-linguistically. Part of this was presented at SULa and APLL 16 ([slides](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1ggQrCSZ2zfRCtcX_D8Ry_xMZFM_dYg5f/view?usp=sharing)).

### Differential object marking

With colleagues at Boston University, we investigated several predictors concerning the distribution of the differential object marker _-(i)g_ in Khalkha Mongolian during our Field Methods class ([slides](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1FwZr6shPjz4VENuPxoe9UXml5CzEkiFJ/view?usp=sharing)).

### _kse-_ prefixation

I investigate a peculiar prefix in Modern Greek, which does several of the things for which English has different prefixes, like _un-_, _de-_, etc. In doing so, I argue for a single lexical semantics entry and different syntactic heights of attachment.
